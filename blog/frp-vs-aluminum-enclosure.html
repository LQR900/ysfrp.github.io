Fiberglass vs. Aluminum Enclosures: Which One Protects Better?

Oct 27, 2026 6 min read

Aluminum has been the default enclosure material for decades, and for good reason: it is strong, machinable, and easy to source. But in coastal, chemical, and outdoor electrical applications, fiberglass reinforced plastic (FRP) enclosures have quietly taken over large parts of the market. If you are specifying housings for telecom cabinets, control panels, EV charging stations, or sensor boxes, the fiberglass-vs-aluminum decision affects corrosion performance, installed weight, RF behavior, and total cost for the next 15–20 years. Here is how the two materials really compare.

1. Corrosion: Where Aluminum Loses Ground

Aluminum is not rust-prone the way carbon steel is, but it is far from immune outdoors. Two mechanisms cause most aluminum enclosure failures:

Galvanic corrosion. Aluminum sits at the active end of the galvanic series. When an aluminum housing is bolted to a stainless steel frame or a galvanized tower and moisture bridges the joint, the aluminum becomes the sacrificial anode and corrodes preferentially — exactly at the mounting points, which are the structural critical zones. Marine and coastal installations fight this with isolating washers, sacrificial anodes, and coatings, all of which add cost and maintenance points.

Pitting and crevice corrosion. Chlorides from salt spray attack the passive oxide layer, creating pits that grow under gaskets and flange edges where moisture stays trapped.

FRP, being a non-metallic composite, simply does not participate in electrochemical reactions. There is no oxide layer to break down, no galvanic couple with stainless fasteners or steel racks, and no need for anodizing, painting, or sacrificial protection. A quality FRP enclosure in a coastal environment is a wash-down-and-forget item: the resin system (isophthalic polyester, vinyl ester, or epoxy) does the corrosion fighting, not a coating that can be scratched off.

2. Weight and Strength-to-Weight

Typical E-glass FRP laminates run 1.5–1.9 g/cm³, versus 2.70 g/cm³ for 6061-T6 aluminum — roughly a 30–45% weight saving at equal volume. Wall-for-wall, an FRP housing is noticeably lighter, which matters more than buyers expect:

  • Installation. A wall-mount cabinet a technician can lift and align alone is faster and safer to install than one needing two people or a lifting aid.
  • Pole and tower loading. On telecom masts and light poles, every kilogram of enclosure weight is subtracted from the payload budget for antennas and mounts.
  • Freight. For OEM volumes shipped internationally, lighter housings directly reduce per-unit logistics cost.

On absolute strength, aluminum still wins: 6061-T6 has a tensile strength around 310 MPa versus 100–300 MPa for typical FRP laminates. But strength-to-weight is close to parity, and FRP can be reinforced locally — extra plies at hinge and latch zones, bonded metal inserts at mounting points — where loads concentrate, without thickening the whole shell.

3. Electrical and RF Behavior

This is the most under-appreciated difference. FRP is a natural electrical insulator. Around live components, an FRP enclosure adds a layer of protection against accidental contact and stray current — there is no conductive enclosure to become energized if internal insulation fails. For grounding-sensitive electronics, that also means no induced ground loops through the housing itself.

FRP is also transparent to radio frequencies. Antenna systems, GNSS receivers, radar sensors, and wireless gateways can often be mounted behind an FRP panel with minimal signal degradation, letting you consolidate radome and enclosure functions in one housing. Aluminum blocks RF completely — any antenna inside an aluminum box needs a purpose-designed window or external mounting, with every feedthrough becoming a potential water-ingress point.

The flip side: when you want electromagnetic shielding, aluminum provides it for free, while FRP needs optional conductive coatings or shielding gaskets. If EMI containment is your primary requirement, that asymmetry matters.

4. Thermal Behavior

Aluminum conducts heat well — helpful if you are dumping controller heat into ambient air. But the same conductivity works against you under direct sun: an aluminum cabinet in full solar load transfers heat inward efficiently, and internal temperatures can exceed what electronics tolerate without active cooling. FRP's low thermal conductivity slows heat transfer in both directions, keeping internal temperatures more stable; quality systems are specified from -40°C to +80°C with UV-stabilized gel coat. For solar-exposed installations, FRP usually reduces the cooling load; for high-dissipation electronics, plan ventilation or heat exchangers regardless of material.

5. Total Cost of Ownership

Sticker-price comparisons between the two materials are misleading. What actually drives cost over a 15–20 year service life:

  • Finishing. Aluminum enclosures for outdoor use need anodizing or powder coating, applied and maintained. FRP's color is in the gel coat — no painting line, no repaint cycle.
  • Maintenance. Coated aluminum in corrosive environments lives on an inspection-and-repair cycle: coating touch-ups, fastener checks, anode replacement. FRP needs periodic cleaning and seal checks.
  • Repairability. A cracked aluminum panel requires welding skills and equipment. FRP damage is patched on site with resin and glass cloth by general maintenance staff.
  • Tooling at volume. Molded FRP amortizes mold cost across the production run, which for OEM volumes often brings the per-unit price close to — or below — fabricated and finished aluminum.

Where Aluminum Still Makes Sense

An honest comparison has to state the counter-cases. Aluminum remains the better choice when the enclosure is primarily a structural member carrying heavy loads or impact exposure; when intrinsic EMI shielding is a hard requirement and coatings are unwelcome; when operating temperatures run beyond composite resin limits; or when end-of-life recyclability dominates the specification. FRP wins most decisively in the environments that destroy coatings: coastal and offshore, chemical plants, wastewater treatment, and roadside installations with de-icing salt.

Quick Selection Checklist

  • Coastal, chemical, or wash-down environment → FRP
  • Antenna, GNSS, or radar inside or behind the housing → FRP
  • Pole-mount or payload-weight-limited installation → FRP
  • No painting or repaint cycle allowed → FRP
  • EMI shielding as a hard requirement → aluminum (or FRP with conductive coating)
  • High structural load or impact exposure → aluminum
  • Uncertain → send both materials your worst-case environment spec and compare qualified answers, not catalog claims
