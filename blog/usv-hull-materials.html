USV Hull Materials: FRP vs Aluminum vs Steel

Nov 23, 2026 7 min read

Every USV specification starts with the same question: what should the hull be made of? The answer drives payload capacity, mission range, sensor performance and maintenance cost for the entire service life of the vessel. Aluminum has been the default for small craft, steel for larger displacement hulls, and fiber reinforced polymer (FRP) has quietly taken over a large share of the unmanned market — especially in the 0.5–5 m class where most commercial and research USVs operate. Here is an honest, numbers-first comparison of the three materials.

1. Weight: Where Payload and Range Are Won

For an unmanned vessel, hull weight is dead weight — every kilogram not spent on structure is available for batteries, sensors and thrusters. Typical densities: E-glass FRP laminates run 1.5–1.9 g/cm³ and carbon fiber laminates 1.5–1.6 g/cm³, versus 2.70 g/cm³ for 6061-T6 aluminum and 7.85 g/cm³ for carbon steel.

At equal volume, an FRP hull weighs roughly 30–45% less than aluminum and about a quarter of an equivalent steel hull. That difference compounds across the whole energy budget: lighter displacement means less thrust to reach planing speed, longer endurance on the same battery pack, and easier launch-and-recovery from a mothership or slipway. For long-endurance survey USVs, foam sandwich FRP construction pushes the advantage further by adding buoyancy without adding structure.

2. Corrosion: The Saltwater Test

Steel hulls must be painted and maintained on a strict inspection cycle — any coating scratch becomes a rust point. Aluminum avoids rust but fights its own battle: chlorides attack the passive oxide layer, causing pitting and crevice corrosion under gaskets and fasteners, and aluminum coupled to stainless hardware becomes the sacrificial anode in a galvanic pair.

GFRP simply does not participate in electrochemistry — no oxide layer to break down, no coating to maintain. One caveat applies to carbon fiber: CFRP is electrically conductive and forms a galvanic couple with metal fittings in saltwater, so metallic hardware on a CFRP hull needs isolation (coatings, washers or bonded insulators). This is a solved engineering problem, but it must be designed in from the start.

3. Stiffness and Strength: What the Numbers Actually Say

Metals win on absolute stiffness: steel has an elastic modulus around 200 GPa, aluminum 69 GPa, while GFRP laminates run 20–40 GPa and standard-modulus CFRP 70–180 GPa. A metal panel of the same thickness deflects less than a GFRP panel — no honest comparison can skip this.

FRP designs close the gap two ways. First, stiffening is placed where the load is: foam sandwich construction turns two thin laminates into a deep, light structural section, dramatically increasing flexural stiffness at almost no weight penalty. Second, on a strength-per-kilogram basis, composites compete well — GFRP tensile strength runs 100–300 MPa against 310 MPa for 6061-T6 and 400 MPa for A36 steel, and CFRP reaches 600–1,500 MPa. Damage behavior also differs: an FRP hull punctures locally and can be patched with resin and cloth, while metal hulls tend to dent and deform over a wider area, requiring welding skills to repair properly.

4. EMI and Sensor Performance

This is where unmanned platforms differ most from crewed boats. A USV is a floating sensor package: radar, GNSS antennas, radios and cameras all live on or inside the hull. FRP is transparent to radio frequencies and does not distort onboard electronics — antennas can work through or behind composite panels without ground-plane complications. FRP hulls also present a lower radar cross-section than metal hulls, which matters for defense applications.

Metal hulls shield RF completely: every antenna needs external mounting or a designed window, and each feedthrough becomes a potential water-ingress point. The flip side — if your mission specifically requires electromagnetic containment, a metal hull provides it for free while FRP needs conductive coatings.

5. Construction: Single Skin vs Foam Sandwich

6. Where Steel and Aluminum Still Make Sense

An honest comparison has to state the counter-cases. Steel remains the right call for large displacement hulls, ice-class or high-impact service, and projects where raw material cost dominates and welding infrastructure already exists. Aluminum wins when the build needs welded fabrication on a fast timeline, intrinsic EMI shielding, or when the platform is large enough that metal framing is more economical than a composite mold. In practice, composites dominate the 0.5–5 m unmanned class — exactly where payload fraction, endurance and sensor performance outweigh raw material cost.

8. Quick Selection Checklist

  • 01Long endurance, battery-driven missions → GFRP or CFRP foam sandwich
  • 02GNSS, radar or comms antennas on board → FRP (RF transparent)
  • 03Multi-year saltwater deployment, minimal maintenance → GFRP
  • 04High-speed or weight-critical platform → CFRP foam sandwich
  • 05Large displacement hull or impact-heavy service → steel or aluminum
  • 06Uncertain → send both materials your worst-case mission profile and compare qualified answers, not catalog claims
